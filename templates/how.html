How PulseCheck works

A small, transparent machine-learning project: what it is trained on, how well it performs, and where it falls short.

{{ icons.icon('cpu', 22) }} From your answers to a risk estimate

  1. 1. Your answersAge, sex, symptoms, blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar and heart-test results.
  2. 2. CleaningBlank fields are filled with typical values from the training patients (for example the median cholesterol). Numbers are scaled and categories encoded the same way as in training.
  3. 3. The modelOne of three models scores the answers, and the score is calibrated so that "30%" means roughly 30 in 100 similar patients in the data had heart disease.
  4. 4. The cutoffIf the probability is above the model's cutoff, the result is flagged as elevated. The cutoff is deliberately low, so a sick patient is less likely to be missed.

  • Logistic Regression is the baseline: a simple statistical model that weighs each answer.
  • Random Forest averages hundreds of decision trees.
  • XGBoost builds trees one after another, each correcting the last one's mistakes.

Recall is the share of truly sick patients the model catches. Precision is the share of flagged patients who really are sick. Missing a sick patient is worse than a false alarm, so recall comes first. The three models score close together; with only 184 test patients the differences are within noise.

{{ icons.icon('alert', 22) }} Limitations

  • Not a diagnosis. The estimate cannot replace a doctor, an ECG or a stress test.
  • Small, old data. {{ n_patients }} patients from four hospitals, mostly men, all referred for heart tests. Results may not hold for you.
  • "No chest pain" raises the estimate. In this dataset, patients without chest pain who were still examined had a high disease rate (about 79%), because they were referred for other reasons. The model learned that pattern, so it can look backwards for a healthy person.
  • Skipped fields are guessed. Blank answers are replaced with the typical value of the training patients, who were all being examined for heart disease. A blank therefore counts as "average for those patients", which makes the estimate less reliable.
  • Hospital effects. Some measurements were only recorded at some hospitals (Switzerland recorded no cholesterol, and 93% of its patients were sick), so whether a value is missing gives away the hospital. The model never sees the hospital, the least reliable columns were left out, and a blank field is never used as a signal on its own.

{{ icons.icon('shield', 22) }} Privacy

1Anonymous and private

There is no login. Your history is linked to a random ID in a cookie in your browser, so other visitors cannot see it. Nothing is sent to a third party.

2Minimal data

Only the answers the model uses are saved. Height and weight are used to show your BMI and are never stored.

3You stay in control

The History page can delete all your saved predictions at any time, and they are removed automatically after {{ history_days }} days.
